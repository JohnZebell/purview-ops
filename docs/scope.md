# Scope

What each engagement includes and what it does not. Written to answer the question a cold reply actually asks, which is what do I get for the money.

Internal for now. Some of this belongs on the site and some of it is better said on a call.

---

## The audit

**$1,000 for the first three engagements, $3,000 after. Two weeks.**

The introductory rate exists because there are no case studies yet. It is not a discount off a price anyone has paid.

**The credit against month one.** For the first three engagements the full $1,000 credits against month one. It is part of the introductory arrangement and ends with it.

After that, the credit scales to the tier. Foundation credits $1,000, Operator $2,000, Embedded $3,000. A larger credit costs more in dollars and less as a share of what is collected, so it shrinks exactly where the engagement is worth more.

The tier is still decided by what the audit found. The credit is what gets said after that, never before it.

**What happens.** An intake doc goes out first, before anything is looked at. It asks where they think revenue is leaking, in their words, and it goes to whoever on their team has an opinion. Then read-only access to the CRM and whatever else holds revenue data. Then a findings document.

**What gets checked.** Seven things. Countable market size against what they are working. Whether the ICP describes who they have actually won. How many inbound leads got contacted at all and how fast. Records with no owner or an owner who left. Real sales cycle counting deals that stalled. Win rate split by segment. How much of the week goes to fixing things that should have worked.

**What they get.** A findings document with a number attached to each leak, ordered by what to do first, in four groups. Stop the bleeding, take things out, agree on what things mean, then automate.

The findings document also names the tier and says why, since the shape of the engagement comes out of the same evidence as the order of the work.

**What it is not.** Not a strategy deck. Not a tool recommendation. Not a plan to implement anything, though the findings imply one.

**No discovery call required.** Follow-up questions happen in writing.

**It is theirs either way.** If they never speak to Purview again they can hand it to whoever fixes it.

## What every retainer includes

Regardless of tier.

Recorded walkthroughs and writing rather than standing meetings. Findings arrive as something watched on their own schedule and forwarded to whoever was not in the room.

Everything built is documented as an SOP their team can run without Purview.

Every change is instrumented to prove its own output rather than report a green status.

A three month minimum, then month to month. The minimum is not a contract preference. Nothing that matters is measurable in less than a full sales cycle, so a shorter engagement can only be judged on activity, and judging this work on activity is how it gets cut.

### The tier is a finding, not a preference

The audit says which one, the same way it says what to fix first. A company that needs eight days a month and buys four does not get a slower version of the result. It gets a queue that never clears, and the work gets judged on a pace it was never bought at.

A tier can be wrong in both directions. Twelve days a month on a company with two hundred deals a year is time spent building things nobody will use. That gets said as plainly as the other one.

## Foundation, $3,500

Roughly four days a month. One workstream at a time. Weekly recorded walkthrough. No shared channel.

**What it is for.** Fixing what the audit found, in the order the audit said to fix it.

**What gets built.** CRM architecture and field structure. Lead routing with fallback handling. Lifecycle stage design and the definitions behind it. Recovery of stranded records. Reporting that reproduces from the system rather than from a person. Removal of fields, steps, and reports that do not improve a decision.

**What it does not include.** The monitoring layer. Four days a month is enough to build a thing or watch a thing, not both.

**Who it is wrong for.** A company that needs more than one thing fixed at once. Four days a month means a queue, and if the queue is long enough that nothing lands, this is the wrong tier.

---

## Operator, $6,500

Roughly eight days a month. Two workstreams. Twice weekly cadence. Shared channel.

**What it adds.** The monitoring layer, once the foundation underneath it is correct. Scoring model validation. Requalification of records that failed at entry. Account activity signals on deterministic criteria. Attribution chain integrity checked rather than fixed once.

Also the pilot to commercial work, where the buyer is institutional and pilots are the motion.

**Why monitoring is not on Foundation.** A monitor needs something correct to monitor. Watching a broken process produces alerts about a system nobody trusts, which is worse than no alerts.

---

## Embedded, $9,500

Roughly twelve days a month. Continuous. Multiple workstreams at once.

**What it adds.** Enough time to build and maintain simultaneously, which is what the first two tiers trade against each other. Full monitoring coverage. Structural work like buyer-type-segmented pipelines, where a company selling to a utility, a corporate, and a co-op needs different required fields and different stages rather than one blended funnel.

**Who it is for.** A company where the revenue system is complicated enough that a queue is the constraint rather than the work itself.

## What no tier includes

Naming these matters more than it costs.

**Running the revenue org.** No standups, no pipeline reviews as a participant, no managing anyone. Purview builds the systems and says what the data shows.

**Being on sales calls.** Not a sales consultant and not a closer.

**Compensation, quota, and territory design.** Adjacent and not this.

**Pricing and packaging strategy.** Same.

**Demand generation strategy, media buying, and content production.** Purview instruments campaigns and does not run them.

**CPQ.** No exposure. Configure, price, quote is a specialty and this is not it.

**Enterprise Salesforce administration at scale.** Validation rules, complex permission models, and governance of large instances. The object model is understood. The admin reps are not there.

**Cold outbound as a service.** Purview builds the system a team runs. It does not become the team.

## Access

**During the audit, read only.** Nothing asked for lets anything change.

**During a retainer, write access only where a build requires it and only for that piece.** Never to a CRM field without a check in front of it, and never as a substitute for a rule.

## Tooling

Every tool with a recurring cost sits on the client's card, in their name, so the system stays theirs when the engagement ends. Purview selects it and runs it.

**Each tier carries a monthly tooling allowance.** Foundation $350. Operator $650. Embedded $950. That is what Purview will put in place without asking. Anything above it is a conversation before it happens rather than a line on an invoice afterward.

The allowance is a ceiling on spend, not a list of tools. No tier is tied to a vendor, and a vendor raising its price does not change what a retainer costs.

**Metered tools sit outside the allowance.** Anything billed by credits, rows, enrichments, or API calls has a cost nobody can predict a month ahead. Those run on the client's account with a usage limit set inside the tool rather than a number agreed on a call. Starbridge meters verified contacts at two credits each with overage at thirteen cents a credit. Clay meters rows. A budget for either is a guess dressed as a number.

---

## The honest version of capacity

Audits can run for anyone who clears the qualifier, which is three or more people selling and a CRM.

Retainers are capacity-limited because they only work with real attention on them. If an audit does not lead to one, that gets said plainly and the findings are still theirs.

---

## One thing to decide before any of this goes on the site

**Whether tier scope goes on the site at all.** Publishing it answers the cold-reply question without a call. It also invites comparison shopping on a list of features, which is the thing every agency page does and yours currently does not. A middle version is publishing what no tier includes and keeping the per-tier build lists for the conversation.
